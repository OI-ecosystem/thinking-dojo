How do I teach my child to think for themselves?

Most advice on this says “encourage curiosity” and stops there, which is hard to act on at half past six on a Tuesday. It gets easier once you see that thinking for yourself is seven smaller skills, and that they build in order.

The Thinking Dojo · 15 November 2026

Why “think about it” doesn’t work

Telling a child to think harder is like telling them to swim faster before anyone has shown them the stroke. They can’t tell which part to change.

The skills also depend on each other. A child who can’t yet describe what happened can’t spot a pattern in it. A child who can’t see the pattern can’t make a good choice about it. So the order matters more than the effort.

The seven skills, in order

The Thinking Dojo gives each skill a belt colour and one plain description. Each has a question you can ask out loud.

  1. Notice · White beltSees what is happening and can describe it clearly.“What did you notice today?”
  2. Pattern · Yellow beltBegins to recognise repetition and simple connections.“What usually happens when you are in a rush?”
  3. Choice · Orange beltMakes simple decisions and starts thinking about what might happen next.“What might happen if you choose this?”
  4. Plan · Green beltThinks ahead and begins to organise actions step by step.“What is your goal?”
  5. Act · Blue beltActs with intention and adjusts while doing.“What is one small step?”
  6. Review · Brown beltUnderstands what happened and begins learning from outcomes.“What would you do differently next time?”
  7. Independent Thinker · Black beltThinks clearly, independently, and with structure.“What do you think, and why?”

How to ask the questions

  • One question, one moment. At dinner, in the car, after a hard day. One is enough.
  • Ask, then wait. The silence is the thinking. If you fill it, you have done the thinking for them.
  • Don’t correct, just listen. Once you fix an answer, the task changes from thinking to guessing what you want to hear.

Where to start

Most children start at Notice, whatever their age. That doesn’t mean they are behind. A twelve-year-old who is quick at maths may still jump straight to what something means without saying what actually happened.

Ask the first question for a week. When they answer it easily, try the next one.

When to move on

There is no test. Move on when you hear your child use a skill without being asked. If they say “this always happens when I leave it until Sunday night”, they have spotted a pattern by themselves, and they are ready for questions about choice.
